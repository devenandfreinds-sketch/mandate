Big Beautiful Bill Ledger
Public Law 119-21 · signed July 4, 2025 · figures as of November 14, 2026

The One Big Beautiful Bill, in numbers

The law cut taxes by about $4.5 trillion over ten years, cut health and food programs by about $1.1 trillion, and gave immigration enforcement its largest budget in history. This page lays out who gains, who loses, what has happened in the first 16 months, and what is still scheduled.

The tax side

Every major tax break, and what paid for part of them

Most of the cost comes from making the 2017 tax cuts permanent. The new breaks for tips, overtime, seniors and car loans are small by comparison and expire after 2028. Bars to the left raise revenue.

Who gains, who loses

The bottom tenth loses $1,214 a year. The top tenth gains $13,622.

The Congressional Budget Office combined the tax cuts with the Medicaid and food-assistance cuts and measured the net effect on each tenth of households. The bottom two tenths end up with less. Everyone from the middle up gains, and the gains grow with income.

The one percent

What the law does for the highest earners

ITEP defines the top 1% in 2026 as households above $960,600, averaging $2.85 million in income. On a taxes-only basis they receive more than $70,000 each this year. They are the only income group still ahead after tariffs and price increases are counted.

  • $70,000+Average 2026 tax cut for the top 1%, against $1,160 for the middle fifth (ITEP).
  • $2.3TTen-year cost of the provisions whose benefits flow mostly to the top 10%: business tax cuts, the pass-through deduction, the top-rate cut, and the estate tax cut (Center for American Progress, from JCT scores).
  • ≈ $1 : $1For each dollar cut from programs serving low-income households, the top 1% receive about a dollar in tax cuts over the decade (CAP analysis of JCT and CBO data, reported by NBC News).
  • $15MEstate tax exemption per person from 2026, $30 million for a couple, made permanent and indexed to inflation. Ten-year cost: $212 billion.
  • 37%Top income tax rate made permanent. It would have returned to 39.6% in 2026.
  • 0–8%Effective federal tax rates ITEP found for Meta, Amazon, Alphabet and Tesla after the law, driven by full expensing of equipment and research.
The president

How Donald Trump stands to benefit

There is no reliable dollar figure for what the law saves Trump personally. His current tax returns are not public, and I found no independent estimate. What can be shown is which provisions fit the way his wealth is held, and separately, how his net worth has changed in office.

Health and food

The cuts arrive slowly, then keep growing

Medicaid loses $911 billion in federal funding over ten years, and nearly two thirds of that lands after 2030. Food assistance changed first: work requirements expanded in November 2025 and enrollment has fallen every month since.

  • 4.1MFewer people on SNAP by March 2026. Arizona alone lost more than 450,000 recipients by May, many of them eligible but caught by new paperwork rules, according to advocates.
  • 700+Rural hospitals at risk of closing within two to three years (Center for Healthcare Quality and Payment Reform). Many were struggling before the law.
  • $50B vs $137BThe law’s rural health fund next to its estimated Medicaid cuts in rural areas (KFF). The fund ends after 2030 and the cuts do not.
  • 996,000Fewer jobs nationwide in 2029 from the Medicaid cuts, half of them in health care (Commonwealth Fund projection).
  • $4.7TProjected addition to the national debt over the decade, including interest, in CBO’s February 2026 outlook.
Immigration enforcement

ICE got seven years of budget in one bill

The law gave the Department of Homeland Security $191 billion. ICE received $74.85 billion to spend through 2029, including $45 billion for detention. By July 2026 it had spent or committed $58 billion of that. Detention, deportations, deaths in custody and shootings by agents all rose. Some of that rise began in early 2025, before the money arrived.

  • 4 citizensU.S. citizens killed by immigration agents in the list above: Ruben Ray Martinez, Keith Porter, Renée Good and Alex Pretti. DHS said agents acted in self-defense in most shootings. In several cases video, witnesses or prosecutors contradicted the official account and charges against the people shot were dropped.
  • 134 → 272Detention facilities in use, January 2025 to July 2026. ICE paid about $1.1 billion for commercial warehouses, then moved to sell seven of them.
  • 1 every 8 daysRate of deaths in ICE detention in the first half of 2026 (Human Rights Watch).
  • 145,000U.S. citizen children with at least one parent taken into ICE custody, January 2025 to April 2026. An estimated 22,000 were left with no parent at home (Brookings).
  • 13,500Cases in which federal judges ruled against ICE’s detention arguments by mid-October 2026 (Politico tally, via MPI).
  • 491,000ICE arrests in fiscal 2026, which DHS reports as an agency record and a result it sought.
The case for it

What supporters point to

The picture above leans on CBO and on critics’ analyses. These are the strongest numbers on the other side.

  • 62%Share of taxpayers who would have faced a tax increase in 2026 if the 2017 cuts had expired (Tax Foundation).
  • $3,813Average tax cut per filer in 2026, counting business cuts, falling to $2,590 in 2030 when temporary breaks end (Tax Foundation).
  • +0.7%Long-run GDP and about 828,000 full-time-equivalent jobs, with growth offsetting 16% of the tax cuts’ cost (Tax Foundation).
  • ~70%Of filers getting a tax cut this year earned under $100,000 (Treasury). That counts people, not dollars.
  • $25,000Maximum deduction for tips, with $12,500 for overtime and $6,000 for seniors, through 2028. A bartender earning $40,000 with $22,000 in tips saves about $2,400 a year (New York Times example).
  • Work rulesSupporters argue the Medicaid and SNAP requirements reserve benefits for people who need them and reduce fraud and improper payments.
Schedule

What has taken effect and what is coming

  • Jul 4, 2025Signed. Tax cuts apply to the 2025 tax year. Homeland security funding becomes available.
  • Sep 30, 2025Electric vehicle tax credits end.
  • Nov 1, 2025SNAP work requirements extend to most adults up to 64, including veterans and homeless people.
  • Jan 1, 2026$15 million estate exemption begins. Enhanced health-insurance credits lapse, which the law did not extend.
  • Jul 1, 2026Federal student loan limits and repayment plans change.
  • Jan 1, 2027Medicaid work requirements begin: 80 hours a month of work, school or volunteering for many adults.
  • Oct 2027States with high error rates start paying 5% to 15% of SNAP benefit costs.
  • Oct 1, 2028Limits on state provider taxes start tightening, squeezing how states fund Medicaid.
  • End of 2028Tips, overtime, senior and car-loan deductions expire.
  • 2030State and local tax deduction cap drops from $40,000 back to $10,000. Rural health fund runs out.
Sources and limits

Projections are estimates and differ by model. CBO is nonpartisan. The Tax Foundation generally favors lower taxes; ITEP and the Center for American Progress lean left. Counts of shootings and custody deaths come from trackers that use different definitions, so totals do not match exactly. The law is not the only cause of what followed: tariffs, the lapsed insurance credits and a second enforcement law in 2026 also shaped these numbers.
